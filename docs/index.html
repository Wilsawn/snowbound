    Strongest between 0.8 and 1.7 km into the loop: 3 m+ with no snow, 1.6 to 2 m in the storm.

    Typical spot: half the spots did better, half did worse. 3 m is about a parking space wide; 0.25 m, the smallest push, about a shoe.

    Room for error: how far off the car's position guess can be and still snap back onto the map. I pushed it off on purpose, 0.25 m to 3 m, at 271 spots on a 20 m grid along 7.4 km of a 7.9 km Toronto loop (kept where both drives had a scan, so mostly 20 to 40 m apart), on both days. 3 m was the biggest push, so "3 m+" means it never failed.

    Smaller at 111 of the 271 spots, the same at 142, bigger at 18. Numbers from the height-locked rerun of 23 November (see The limits).

      [ how I measured it ]

      Push the guess off, let the matcher slide the scan back, count the recoveries.

      Engineers call this the convergence basin: the largest starting error from which the scan matcher (ICP) still finds the right pose.

      1. Build a 3D map of the loop from a drive two months earlier with no snow (26 November 2020), placing each scan with the car's recorded position.
      2. Take a scan from the storm, or from the no-snow day, and start it from a guess that is deliberately off: 0.25 m to 3 m, eight directions, with a 5 degree turn added.
      3. Let the matcher (ICP, point to plane) slide the scan onto the map.
      4. Count it as recovered if it lands within 30 cm and 2 degrees of the truth. The room for error is the biggest push where 7 of 8 directions recover. "3 m+" means every size passed.
      The limits

      271 spots, one drive per kind of weather. 202 of the no-snow spots and 120 of the storm spots hit the 3 m cap, so the real gap is at least what the numbers show. 12 spots fail at the smallest push on both days: that is the method, not the snow. The last 400 m is hard on both days: that is the road, not the weather. Every value is good to about half a metre.

      Fixed 23 November. The matcher used to be free to move the scan up and down as well as sideways. In the storm that let it sink a moving scan about 2.5 m so the snow in the air landed on the road, and the test counted a failure that was not one. Every number on this page is now from a rerun of all 271 spots with the height locked: the matcher may only slide and turn. The lock changed 101 storm values and 17 no-snow values. The count of spots where snow cut the room went from 147 to 111, the typical values did not move (storm 2.5 m, no snow 3 m+), and 18 storm spots still read 0 m, 12 of them on both days. The free-matcher numbers stay in the results folder for comparison.

      The two days differ in more than falling snow: a week of parked cars and snowbanks, and the no-snow car drove faster. The map is thinner over most of the loop than on the first street I measured; re-running that street gave the same conclusion but storm values about one step smaller. The result holds for these matcher settings; a wider search window recovers more often and makes the numbers bigger.

      The dashcam frames are untouched apart from a light grade on the opening photo; the falling snow drawn over that photo is an effect, not data. The lidar scans are real scans with only the car's own body (within 3 m) removed; the two 3D scans in the opening are 200,000 points each in the files, drawn as 150,000 on a fast computer, 80,000 on most, 40,000 on phones.

      [ what I learned ]

      Snow cuts the room for error, but not everywhere, and the test can fool you.

      At 111 of 271 spots the storm left less room; at 142 it made no difference. The matcher fooled me once, by sinking a storm scan until the snow sat on the road (The limits has the story). The last 400 m is hard on both days: that is the road, not the weather.
